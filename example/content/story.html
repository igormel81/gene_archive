Two families, one from the Atlantic edge of Ireland and one from a village under the Carpathians, crossed the ocean twelve years apart and met in Pittsburgh. This is what the documents and the family's own memories tell us so far. Everyone in this archive is fictional: it is a demonstration of the site engine.

1847 The famine years in Mayo

The oldest Harper we can name is Patrick Harper, a tenant farmer in the parish of Louisburgh, County Mayo, where the land runs out between Croagh Patrick and the sea. He was about fifteen in the black winter of 1847. The family story, told to Kate Harper by her grandmother, is that the Harpers kept alive by breaking stones on the relief road along the coast, and that two of Patrick's younger brothers were buried that spring (S16). No register of those deaths survives, and we have not been able to give the brothers names.

Patrick's age is itself a small puzzle. When he married Bridget Walsh in the parish chapel in February 1856, the priest wrote that he was 22 (S2), which would mean he was born about 1834. The family, remembering how old he was when he died, puts his birth closer to 1832. Both versions are kept on his card until a baptism turns up.

Patrick and Bridget had at least three children who lived. Michael was born in September 1858 and baptised in Louisburgh a few days later, with his mother entered under her maiden name (S1). Mary stayed in Mayo all her life. A Thomas is remembered as 'the brother who went to Liverpool', and a Martin Harper who farmed next door to Bridget in 1901 may be another son (S15). Those two are drawn with dashed lines in the tree: probable, not proven.

1880 Michael sails from Queenstown

In the spring of 1880 Michael, twenty-one and the eldest son, left the farm that should have been his. He went by coach to Castlebar, by train to Cork, and on to Queenstown — today's Cobh — where the liners called on their way from Liverpool to New York. The passenger list gives him as a labourer bound for Pittsburgh (S4). He landed at Castle Garden, the old immigrant depot at the tip of Manhattan; Ellis Island did not open for another twelve years.

Why Pittsburgh? Nobody in the family remembers. One clue is in the census taken that June: Michael was boarding in the Sixth Ward and working at a rolling mill, and two doors away lived Honora Harper, a forty-year-old widow from Ireland who took in laundry (S14). She may have been Patrick's sister, waiting for her nephew. She may equally have been a stranger with the same name. Until we find her death record, she stays on the edge of the tree as a possible relative.

1884 A wedding at St. Patrick's

Four years after he arrived, Michael married Catherine 'Kate' Doyle at St. Patrick's church in the Strip District. Kate had been born in New York in 1862 to Irish parents we cannot yet name, and had come to Pittsburgh with her brother Owen, who stood as witness. The couple went straight from the church to a studio and had their portrait made on a cabinet card. On the back, in Kate's careful hand, is the earliest family writing we have (S3).

Michael spent nearly forty years in the mills and rose to be a roller, a skilled and well-paid job. The family lived in a narrow brick house on Butler Street in Lawrenceville. Kate bore four children; one died as a baby and has left no name in any record we have found. The three who grew up were John Joseph, called Jack, born in 1886; Margaret, called Peg, born in 1889; and Daniel, born on the Fourth of July 1892, which his father took as a very good sign.

Michael died in March 1924. The Pittsburgh paper called him 'a native of the County Mayo and a man well liked on Butler Street' (S10). Kate lived seven more years, and they share one gravestone at Calvary Cemetery (S19).

1892 Josef leaves Kapušany

Twelve years after Michael sailed, and a thousand miles to the east, Jozef Novák set out from Kapušany, a village under a ruined castle on the road north of Prešov. It was then part of the Kingdom of Hungary, and his baptism of March 1866 is written in Latin: Josephus, son of Joannes Novák and Anna Hudák (S6). His parents, Ján and Anna, never left the village.

Like thousands of young men from Sáros county, Josef went to Hamburg, where the emigrant clerks wrote his name into the lists in May 1892. On the same page is a Michal Novák, 23, from the same village (S9). Was he a brother, a cousin, or simply another Novák — one of the commonest names in Slovakia? He is not on the New York manifest beside Josef, and no one in the family remembers him. Josef himself landed at the brand-new Ellis Island station and gave his age as 25 (S5), which is why older family trees, copying the manifest, have him born in 1867.

He went to Chicago, where Slovak boarding houses crowded around the stockyards, and his younger brother Štefan followed a year later. So did Mária Kováčová, a girl from Kapušany whom Josef had known all his life. They married in Chicago in October 1894, with Štefan as a witness (S7). In American papers they became Joseph and Mary Novak.

1898 Coal in Windber

In 1898 Josef took his young family to Windber, a coal town in Somerset County that a mining company had laid out only the year before. He dug coal there for fourteen years, and Mary ran a boarding house for single miners. Their first two children, Frank and Helen, had been born in Chicago; a third, Andrew, was born in Windber in 1900 and died of diphtheria at eighteen months. His burial is one of the few family facts that has reached print, in the jubilee booklet of the Windber Slovak parish (S20). In 1904 Josef became an American citizen (S26).

Next door lived the Sabo family. When Mr. Sabo was killed in the mine in 1907 and his widow died two years later, Mary took their little daughter Rose into her own house. In 1910 the Orphans' Court of Somerset County made it official, and Rose became a Novak (S13). The court file is a two-page petition and decree, and it is the reason Rose appears in the tree as an adopted child rather than a daughter by birth.

The photograph above was taken in 1906 in front of the company houses and posted to Josef's mother in Kapušany. Years later it came back across the ocean with another emigrant from the village, and Helen kept it all her life. In 1912 the Novaks left the coal town for Pittsburgh's South Side, where Josef worked as a watchman at a steel works until his lungs gave out. He died there in August 1931 (S22).

1918 Daniel in France

Michael and Kate's youngest son, Daniel Harper, enlisted in 1917 and went to France with an infantry division from western Pennsylvania. In the autumn of 1918 he was wounded in the Meuse-Argonne offensive. On 2 November the Pittsburgh paper reported that he had been killed on 8 October (S11). The War Department's card, found a century later, says he died of wounds on 12 October (S12). We think the paper was reporting the day he was hit. Both dates are on his card.

His brother Jack registered for the draft that year as a married steelworker with a small son and was never called (S24). That son, James Patrick, was not so lucky in the next war. Jimmy was drafted in 1942 and photographed in uniform at Camp Shelby the following year (S18); he landed in Normandy in June 1944 and was killed nine days later. His sister Margaret had by then moved to Chicago after a divorce that the family did not discuss.

1949 A Harper meets a Novak

The Irish of Lawrenceville and the Slovaks of the South Side lived a river apart and went to different churches. What brought them together was a parish dance. Tom Harper, Jack's second son, home from the navy and working for the electric company, went along with a Slovak friend from work to St. Matthew's hall, and there he met Dorothy Novak, Frank's daughter, a bookkeeper with a famous laugh.

They married at St. Matthew's in June 1949. Tom's mother Nora, a dressmaker, sewed the wedding dress, and for the first time Harpers and Novaks stood together on the church steps (S17). The inscription on the back of the studio portrait sends 'love to Aunt Helen' — Helen Novak, by then a schoolteacher of nearly thirty years' standing, who had never married and had become the keeper of the Novak memories.

Tom and Dottie settled in Brookline and had three children. The eldest, Michael James, was named both for the great-grandfather who had sailed from Queenstown and for the uncle who died in Normandy.

1985 How the archive began

Aunt Helen died in Pittsburgh in 1985, a few months after Michael James's son Daniel was born. Her shoebox of photographs and Christmas cards from the Chicago cousins went to Dorothy. In 1998, the year Tom died, Dorothy sat down with a school notebook and wrote out everything she could remember of what Helen and her parents had told her: Kapušany, the boat from Hamburg, the boarding house in Windber, how Rose came to the family (S25). That notebook is still the backbone of the Novak half of this site.

Michael James began putting the photographs into albums; after his death in 2019 the boxes passed to his son, who started scanning them and checking every story against the records. Kate Harper sat down for a long recorded conversation in 2025 (S16). Since then the parish registers of Louisburgh and Kapušany, the census of 1880 and 1901, the Hamburg lists and a court file from Somerset County have each confirmed, corrected or complicated something the family remembered. Where they disagree, both versions are kept, with the reason for choosing one of them in the corrections log.

Much is still unknown. We do not know where Kate Doyle's parents came from, whether Honora Harper was Michael's aunt, or who Michal Novák was. If you can help with any of these, the Questions page says what we are looking for.
